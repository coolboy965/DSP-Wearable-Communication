%% ── Abstract ───────────────────────────────────────────────
\begin{abstract}
This paper presents the design and hardware realization of a wearable bidirectional communication aid connecting American Sign Language (ASL) users and spoken-language users. Hand postures and motion dynamics are acquired using five finger-mounted flex sensors and a six-axis inertial measurement unit (MPU-6050) sampled at 200~Sa/s by an ARM Cortex-M4 microcontroller (STM32F407VG). High-frequency motion artefacts and mechanical jitter are attenuated using an on-chip 41-tap linear-phase FIR low-pass filter ($f_c = 50$~Hz) designed with a Hamming window. The conditioned time-series data is segmented into 1.0~s windows, from which a 33-dimensional statistical feature vector consisting of root-mean-square (RMS) amplitude, arithmetic mean, and sample variance across all eleven channels is extracted. A minimum Euclidean distance classifier with an empirical rejection boundary maps feature vectors to ten distinct ASL signs, triggering phoneme-based speech synthesis. In the reverse direction, spoken acoustic signals captured by a Knowles MEMS microphone (SPH0645) are conditioned via an IIR digital bandpass filter (300~Hz to 3.4~kHz) and automatic gain control before being delivered to the wearer through a mastoid bone-conduction transducer (Dayton Audio TT25-8). Experimental verification demonstrated a 91.4\% mean gesture classification accuracy across 900 test trials, an acoustic output signal-to-noise ratio of 22~dB, and an active system power draw of 382~mW, providing 14 to 19 hours of battery operation from a 2000~mAh cell.
\end{abstract}

Digital signal processing, FIR low-pass filter, gesture recognition, bone-conduction transducer, embedded systems, assistive technology.

%% ── Section I: Introduction ────────────────────────────────
\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

\IEEEPARstart{S}{ign} language serves as the primary medium of daily communication for more than 70 million deaf individuals globally~\cite{who2021}. While visual-gestural languages convey complete linguistic structures, their vocabulary is unfamiliar to the majority of hearing individuals. In everyday settings such as healthcare clinics, service counters, and workplaces, this asymmetry impedes spontaneous communication in the absence of human interpreters.

Existing technical approaches can be separated into vision-based systems and wearable sensor gloves. Video-based gesture recognition algorithms rely on external cameras, requiring stable illumination, unobstructed optical line-of-sight, and significant processing power. In contrast, sensor-equipped gloves provide a self-contained capture mechanism unaffected by ambient lighting or user occlusions~\cite{mehdi2002}. Most prior prototypes, however, operate strictly unidirectionally: they translate sign gestures into text or synthetic speech, but provide no integrated pathway for the hearing user's spoken response to reach the deaf individual.

This project implements a bidirectional wearable communication platform running real-time digital signal processing on an embedded processor. The system addresses both channels simultaneously:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Forward Path (Sign-to-Speech):} Five finger-bend flex sensors and a wrist-mounted six-axis inertial measurement unit (IMU) measure hand configuration. The raw readings pass through a linear-phase FIR low-pass filter to reject noise and tremor artefacts. A minimum-distance classifier identifies gesture postures from statistical features and drives an audio amplifier to voice the translated sign.
  \item \textbf{Reverse Path (Speech-to-Bone-Conduction):} A digital MEMS microphone samples ambient speech at 16~kHz. The audio signal is constrained to the 300~Hz--3.4~kHz telephone speech band via an IIR filter and dynamic automatic gain control, driving a bone-conduction transducer placed against the temporal bone. This mechanism transmits acoustic vibrations to the inner ear without blocking the external auditory canal.
\end{enumerate}

%% ── Section II: Related Work ───────────────────────────────
\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

Early research on dataglove interfaces by Fels and Hinton~\cite{fels1993} utilized multilayer neural networks for hand posture recognition. While achieving high accuracy on a 131-sign vocabulary, their setup required stationary workstations, limiting portability. Oz and Leu~\cite{oz2011} integrated sensory gloves with thresholding and multi-channel classifiers for American Sign Language words, noting that pre-classification noise filtering directly impacts accuracy across repetitive trials.

Marin et al.~\cite{marin2014} demonstrated that fusing flex-sensor bend signals with three-axis accelerometers significantly resolves ambiguities between signs that share identical finger curvature but differ in hand tilt (such as the letters `D' and `F'). Reinfeldt et al.~\cite{reinfeldt2010} characterized the electromechanical transfer function of human skull bone-conduction paths, establishing that frequencies between 300~Hz and 3.5~kHz provide sufficient spectral energy for consonant and vowel identification while minimizing transducer power requirements.

%% ── Section III: System Architecture ───────────────────────
\section{System Architecture}
\label{sec:arch}

The system architecture consists of two concurrent digital signal processing pipelines executing on a single microcontroller unit (MCU), illustrated in Fig.~\ref{fig:sysarch}.

\begin{figure*}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.92\textwidth]{images/system_architecture.eps}
\caption{Overall bidirectional system architecture. The forward path (top) captures hand posture via flex sensors and an IMU, filtering and classifying gestures to drive speech synthesis. The reverse path (bottom) captures external speech, filtering and delivering vibrations via a bone-conduction transducer.}
\label{fig:sysarch}
\end{figure*}

\subsection{Forward Pipeline: Gesture-to-Speech}
The forward subsystem incorporates five resistive flex sensors positioned along each finger and an MPU-6050 IMU tracking wrist angular velocity and linear acceleration. The MCU samples five analog channels through its 12-bit SAR ADC at 200~Sa/s, while receiving six degrees-of-freedom inertial data over I$^2$C at 400~kHz. The resulting eleven data streams are filtered through an on-chip 41-tap FIR filter to eliminate mechanical vibrations above 50~Hz.

A sliding 1.0~s buffer (200 samples per channel) aggregates the conditioned signals. At each window termination, the feature extractor computes root-mean-square (RMS) energy, arithmetic mean, and sample variance across each channel, assembling a 33-element vector. The nearest-neighbor classifier compares this vector against calibrated prototype templates. Upon successful classification, the MCU issues control tokens to a speech synthesis module, which outputs audio via a PAM8403 class-D amplifier driving a dynamic speaker.

\subsection{Reverse Pipeline: Speech-to-Bone-Conduction}
The reverse path captures conversational acoustic input through a Knowles SPH0645LM4H MEMS microphone. The microphone outputs pulse-density modulated digital audio transferred into MCU memory using the I$^2$S peripheral at 16~kHz with 24-bit resolution. An IIR bandpass filter attenuates subsonic wind noise ($<300$~Hz) and ambient high-frequency hiss ($>3.4$~kHz). An automatic gain control (AGC) stage dynamically scales the signal to $-18$~dBFS. The processed digital audio stream exits through the MCU's 12-bit DAC, where a linear amplifier drives a Dayton Audio TT25-8 bone-conduction transducer positioned on the user's mastoid bone.

\subsection{Sensor Interface Circuitry}
Each flex sensor is incorporated into an analog voltage divider with a 47~k$\Omega$ precision reference resistor:
\begin{equation}
V_{\text{out}} = V_{\text{DD}} \cdot \frac{R_{\text{flex}}}{R_{\text{flex}} + 47\text{ k}\Omega}
\label{eq:vdiv}
\end{equation}
When a finger is straight ($R_{\text{flex}} \approx 10\text{ k}\Omega$), $V_{\text{out}}$ equals 0.58~V. When fully flexed at 90 degrees ($R_{\text{flex}} \approx 110\text{ k}\Omega$), $V_{\text{out}}$ rises to 2.31~V. This 1.73~V dynamic range fits comfortably inside the 0~V to 3.3~V conversion window of the ADC.

\subsection{Microcontroller Selection}
The STM32F407VG provides single-precision hardware floating-point support via ARM Cortex-M4 instructions, executing single-cycle multiply-accumulate operations (MAC). The processor houses three independent SAR ADCs, an I$^2$S interface with DMA capability, two 12-bit DACs, and 1~MB of on-chip flash memory, sufficient to hold filter coefficients and sign prototypes without external memory chips.

\section{DSP Theory and Mathematical Formulations}
\label{sec:dsp_theory}

Fig.~\ref{fig:dsppipe} diagrams the mathematical operations converting sensor inputs to classified gestures.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{images/dsp_pipeline.eps}
\caption{Detailed signal conditioning, segmentation, and classification pipeline executed on the microcontroller.}
\label{fig:dsppipe}

\subsection{Quantization and Sampling Parameters}
The 12-bit ADC converts voltage levels with a quantization step:
\begin{equation}
\Delta = \frac{V_{\text{ref}}}{2^N} = \frac{3.3\text{ V}}{4096} = 0.8057\text{ mV}
\label{eq:qstep}
\end{equation}
Assuming a uniform distribution of quantization errors over $[-\Delta/2, +\Delta/2]$, the quantization noise power is:
\begin{equation}
P_q = \frac{\Delta^2}{12} = \frac{(0.8057 \times 10^{-3})^2}{12} = 5.41 \times 10^{-8}\text{ V}^2
\label{eq:qnoise}
\end{equation}
With voluntary finger motions bounded below 15~Hz, sampling at $f_s = 200$~Sa/s provides a Nyquist bandwidth of 100~Hz, well above the signal spectrum.

\subsection{FIR Low-Pass Filter Design}
A discrete-time linear-phase finite impulse response (FIR) filter suppresses involuntary muscle tremor and electrical noise. The filter output is given by:
\begin{equation}
y[n] = \sum_{k=0}^{M} h[k] \cdot x[n-k]
\label{eq:fir_conv}
\end{equation}
where $M = 40$ denotes the filter order (41 taps). To ensure strictly linear phase across all passband components, symmetric impulse response coefficients $h[k] = h[M-k]$ were applied. The constant group delay of this filter is:
\begin{equation}
\tau_g = \frac{M}{2} = 20\text{ samples} = \frac{20}{200\text{ Sa/s}} = 100\text{ ms}
\label{eq:group_delay}
\end{equation}

The coefficients were generated through the windowed-sinc technique using an ideal low-pass response $h_d[k]$ weighted by a Hamming window $w[k]$:
\begin{equation}
h_d[k] = \frac{\sin\!\left(2\pi \frac{f_c}{f_s}\left(k - \frac{M}{2}\right)\right)}{\pi \left(k - \frac{M}{2}\right)}, \quad k = 0, 1, \dots, M
\label{eq:sinc}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
w[k] = 0.54 - 0.46 \cos\!\left(\frac{2\pi k}{M}\right), \quad 0 \le k \le M
\label{eq:hamming}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
h[k] = \frac{h_d[k] \cdot w[k]}{\sum_{j=0}^{M} h_d[j] \cdot w[j]}
\label{eq:norm_coeff}
\end{equation}
For $f_c = 50$~Hz and $f_s = 200$~Hz, the transition band spans approximately $\Delta f = 8 f_s / M = 40$~Hz. Fig.~\ref{fig:fir_response} shows the frequency response of this filter.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{images/fir_frequency_response.eps}
\caption{Magnitude frequency response of the 41-tap Hamming-windowed FIR filter. The response remains flat ($<0.5$~dB ripple) up to 40~Hz and achieves $>42$~dB attenuation beyond 60~Hz.}
\label{fig:fir_response}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Feature Vector Construction}
Following FIR filtering, each sensor stream is segmented into 1.0~s blocks ($N = 200$ samples). Three statistical metrics are computed for each channel $i \in \{1, 2, \dots, 11\}$:
\begin{align}
\bar{x}_i &= \frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1} x_i[n] \label{eq:mean} \\
\sigma_i^2 &= \frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1} \bigl(x_i[n] - \bar{x}_i\bigr)^2 \label{eq:var} \\
x_{\text{rms},i} &= \sqrt{\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1} x_i^2[n]} \label{eq:rms}
\end{align}
The combined 33-dimensional feature vector is defined as:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{f} = \begin{bmatrix} x_{\text{rms},1}, \bar{x}_1, \sigma^2_1, \dots, x_{\text{rms},11}, \bar{x}_{11}, \sigma^2_{11} \end{bmatrix}^T
\label{eq:fvec}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Classification Decision Rule}
During calibration, reference vectors $\mathbf{p}_g \in \mathbb{R}^{33}$ are recorded for each sign $g \in \{1, 2, \dots, 10\}$. At runtime, the squared Euclidean distance is evaluated:
\begin{equation}
D_g^2 = \|\mathbf{f} - \mathbf{p}_g\|_2^2 = \sum_{j=1}^{33} (f_j - p_{g,j})^2
\label{eq:dist}
\end{equation}
The predicted gesture $\hat{g}$ is selected by:
\begin{equation}
\hat{g} = \arg\min_{g \in \{1,\dots,10\}} D_g^2
\label{eq:argmin}
\end{equation}
To prevent erroneous triggers during intermediate hand transitions, an empirical distance rejection gate $\tau$ is applied:
\begin{equation}
\text{Output} = \begin{cases} \hat{g}, & \text{if } D_{\hat{g}} \le \tau \\ \text{REJECT}, & \text{if } D_{\hat{g}} > \tau \end{cases}
\label{eq:rejection}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Speech Filtering and Automatic Gain Control}
In the speech path, incoming audio is processed through cascaded second-order direct-form II transposed biquads representing a 2nd-order Butterworth bandpass filter:
\begin{equation}
y[n] = b_0 x[n] + d_1[n-1]
\label{eq:biquad1}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
d_1[n] = b_1 x[n] - a_1 y[n] + d_2[n-1]
\label{eq:biquad2}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
d_2[n] = b_2 x[n] - a_2 y[n]
\label{eq:biquad3}
\end{equation}
The AGC unit calculates signal energy over 10~ms intervals ($L = 160$ samples at 16~kHz):
\begin{equation}
E_k = \sqrt{\frac{1}{L}\sum_{m=0}^{L-1} y^2[kL + m]}
\label{eq:energy}
\end{equation}
The tracking gain factor $G_k$ is updated smoothly:
\begin{equation}
G_{k+1} = 0.90 \cdot G_k + 0.10 \cdot \left(\frac{V_{\text{target}}}{E_k}\right)
\label{eq:gain_smooth}
\end{equation}
clamped within $[0.1, 10.0]$ to prevent clipping on acoustic transients.

%% ── Section VI: Implementation ─────────────────────────────
\section{Implementation Details}
\label{sec:impl}

Listing~\ref{lst:fir} presents the C implementation of the circular-buffer FIR filter in Q15 fixed-point arithmetic on the STM32F4 processor.

\begin{lstlisting}[language=C, caption={Embedded C routine executing circular-buffer FIR convolution.}, label={lst:fir}]
#include <stdint.h>
#define FIR_TAPS 41

static const int16_t H_Q15[FIR_TAPS] = {
   -21,  -46,  -58,  -41,   19,  122,  246,  346,  364,  248,
    -2, -346, -705, -975,-1022, -752, -104,  870, 2049, 3221,
  4096, 3221, 2049,  870, -104, -752,-1022, -975, -705, -346,
    -2,  248,  364,  346,  246,  122,   19,  -41,  -58,  -46,
   -21
};

int16_t fir_update(int16_t *delay, uint8_t *idx, int16_t x) {
    int32_t acc = 0;
    delay[*idx] = x;
    for (uint8_t i = 0; i < FIR_TAPS; i++) {
        uint8_t tap = (*idx + FIR_TAPS - i) % FIR_TAPS;
        acc += (int32_t)H_Q15[i] * (int32_t)delay[tap];
    }
    *idx = (*idx + 1) % FIR_TAPS;
    return (int16_t)(acc >> 15);
}
\end{lstlisting}

Evaluating 33 features across 10 prototype templates requires 330 multiply-accumulate operations, completing in under 18~$\mu$s on the 168~MHz Cortex-M4 core.

As shown in Table~\ref{tab:accuracy}, average recognition accuracy reached 91.4\%. The primary classification confusion occurred between letters `D' and `F', which exhibit similar finger positions. Incorporating wrist pitch and roll orientation from the MPU-6050 reduced confusion on these classes from 14\% to 6\%.

\subsection{Speech and Acoustic Output Quality}
Acoustic performance of the Dayton TT25-8 transducer was tested with speech samples played into the Knowles microphone under ambient laboratory noise (65~dB SPL). The bone-conduction path delivered an audio signal-to-noise ratio of 22~dB. Subjective intelligibility testing across five listeners produced a Word Error Rate (WER) of 8.4\%, demonstrating functional speech intelligibility.

\subsection{Latency and Power Analysis}
Total latency from gesture completion to speech output measured 1.12~s, dominated by the 1.0~s observation window. Power consumption measured 382~mW in continuous active mode, enabling approximately 14 to 19 hours of battery runtime on a 2000~mAh lithium-polymer cell.

%% ── Section VIII: Conclusion ───────────────────────────────
\section{Conclusion and Future Scope}
\label{sec:conclusion}

A wearable communication system providing bidirectional translation between sign gestures and spoken words was designed, constructed, and evaluated. A 41-tap linear-phase FIR low-pass filter implemented on an STM32F407 microcontroller achieved over 43~dB stopband attenuation, removing sensor noise while maintaining constant group delay. A 33-dimensional statistical feature representation coupled with a minimum Euclidean distance classifier achieved an average recognition rate of 91.4\% across ten ASL signs. The reverse bone-conduction audio channel delivered speech with an output SNR of 22~dB without occluding the auditory canal.

Future work will expand the sign vocabulary to full alphanumeric and continuous phrase recognition using support vector machines (SVM) or lightweight convolutional networks, alongside dynamic windowing to decrease end-to-end latency below 500~ms.
